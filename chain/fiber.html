Stage 1: the fiber, and what it delivers

A 0.45% index step, one surviving mode, and the 10.4 micrometre spot that everything downstream has to be designed around. Includes an interactive solver for the exact mode, and an investigation into why the datasheet and the textbook disagree.

A thread of glass with a slightly denser thread down its middle. Light travelling nearly straight along that middle strikes the boundary at a very shallow angle, and beyond a certain shallowness it cannot cross, so it is reflected back in. Repeat that forever and the light is trapped, held not by a wall but by an index difference smaller than half a percent.

Everything in this stage is bookkeeping about two things: how shallow "shallow enough" is, and how wide the trapped light ends up being. The second number is the one that leaves this stage and constrains the next.

NA is the index step, rewritten

Numerical aperture is not an extra property of the fiber. It is the index difference expressed as an angle, and the derivation is three lines.

At the core-cladding boundary, total internal reflection requires the internal angle to exceed the critical angle, sinθc = n₂/n₁. A ray entering the flat end face at θa in air refracts to θr inside, with sinθa = n₁ sinθr. The angle it then makes at the side boundary is 90° − θr, so the condition becomes cosθr > n₂/n₁. Combining:

sin θa < n₁ · √(1 − n₂²/n₁²) = √(n₁² − n₂²) ≡ NA

Run it backwards for SMF-28. With NA = 0.14 and n₁ ≈ 1.468, and using NA² = n₁² − n₂² ≈ 2·n₁·Δn:

Δn ≈ 0.0067. A single-mode fiber carries light across an ocean on an index step of less than half a percent. That is also why it is so sensitive to bending: there is very little index difference holding the light in.
The acceptance-cone picture that this derivation rests on is honest only for multimode fiber. A single-mode fiber has exactly one mode; there are no rays to accept or reject. NA survives as a useful quantity not because of the cone but because it feeds the next number.

V asks how much room there is for modes

Light bouncing along the core is not free to take any angle. Travelling out to the boundary and back has to reproduce its own phase, or the wave cancels itself. Only a discrete set of angles survives, and those are the modes.

V = 2π · a · NA / λ

Read it as three factors rather than a formula to memorise. 2π/λ is the wavenumber, phase per micrometre. a is how far across the guide is. NA is the steepest angle allowed. Their product is how much transverse phase the guide can accommodate, which is exactly how much room there is for modes to fit.

Shrink the core, weaken the step, or lengthen the wavelength and V falls until only one pattern still fits. Below 2.405, one. That number is the first zero of the Bessel function J₀, which is precisely where the second pattern first becomes able to fit.

Marcuse turns V into a size

w/a = 0.65 + 1.619·V−3/2 + 2.879·V−6

This is not derived from anything. The exact mode is a Bessel function J₀ inside the core joined to a modified Bessel function K₀ outside, through an eigenvalue equation with no closed-form solution. Marcuse fitted a Gaussian to the exact result and published the fit, accurate to about 1% for 1.2 < V < 2.4.

Read the three terms as behaviour. The 0.65 is the floor: hold the mode tightly enough and it settles to a fixed fraction of the core. The V−3/2 term is the main one, the mode spreading into the cladding as V falls. The V−6 term is the panic term: approach cutoff and the mode balloons, which is why a fiber run too near its cutoff wavelength becomes hopelessly bend-sensitive.

Its purpose is entirely practical. Once modes are Gaussians, the coupling integral between two of them has a closed form. Every loss formula used later in this chain exists because of this one approximation, and it comes from the same 1977 paper as those formulas.

Common slip: V−3/2 means 1/(V·√V). At V = 2.33 that term is 0.457, not 0.257. A useful check on the result is that w/a must come out greater than 1. The mode is wider than the core. A value below 1 means the exponent was mishandled.

The mode explorer solves the exact eigenvalue equation in the browser and plots the true Bessel mode against Marcuse's Gaussian, on linear and logarithmic scales, with wavelength and NA as sliders. Everything in the next two sections was measured with it.

The datasheet does not agree with the model

Corning's SMF-28 datasheet gives core diameter 8.2 µm, NA 0.14, and mode field diameter 10.4 µm at 1550 nm. Feed the first two into the model at 1550 nm:

V = 2.326, single mode ✓
w/a = 1.125, so w = 4.61 µm and MFD = 9.22 µm
against a specified 10.4 µm. The model is 11% low.

Worse, the same datasheet contains a second number that can be checked independently: the cable cutoff wavelength, specified at 1260 nm or below. Cutoff means V = 2.405, so

λc = 2π · a · NA / 2.405 = 2π × 4.1 × 0.14 / 2.405 = 1500 nm

1500 nm would mean SMF-28 is not single mode at 1310, and it plainly is, since the entire access network runs on it there. So NA = 0.14 is not merely imprecise for mode calculations; it is contradicted by another line on its own datasheet.

Two independent routes to the real NA

Neither route uses the quoted NA. Both use a different specified quantity and solve backwards for the NA that would produce it.

RouteTargetNA it implies
Cutoffλc = 1260 nm0.116 read off the slider, 0.1177 solved exactly
Mode sizeMFD = 10.4 µm at 1550 nm0.120

They agree within 3%, and they agree in the expected direction: the cutoff-derived value comes out slightly lower because cable cutoff is measured on deployed fiber with bends in it, and bending strips the second mode early, so a cabled measurement always reads low. The physical NA governing the mode is about 0.118.

A datasheet value is the result of a measurement made under a stated convention, not a physical constant that can be dropped into any equation. There are at least three quantities called numerical aperture for this fiber and at least three called mode field diameter, and they differ by 15 to 20%. Each definition exists because it is the one a particular instrument can measure reliably.

The engineering rule: never mix a spec from one convention into a formula built on another, and when the convention cannot be established, do not use the number as a design input. Measure it under the convention your model needs, or design against a number whose provenance you know.

Why bends matter

The mode extends beyond the core. Now bend the fiber. The whole mode must travel around the curve keeping one common phase front, like a rank of soldiers wheeling in formation. The part of the mode on the outside of the bend sits at a larger radius, so to keep up it would have to move faster. Past a certain distance from the axis it would have to exceed the speed of light in the cladding, which it cannot. That portion stops being guided and radiates away.

Bend loss is the tail being amputated. The further the mode extends from the core, the sooner the amputation begins, which is why the same fiber bends worse at 1550 nm than at 1310 nm.

Solved with NA = 0.118, the fraction of guided power travelling outside the core is 19% at 1310 nm and 27% at 1550 nm. Same core, same glass, half as much again outside it.

This is also where the Gaussian approximation fails hardest. On a logarithmic plot the true mode falls off along a straight line, dying as exp(−r), while the Gaussian curves away downward, dying as exp(−r²). By 20 µm they differ by orders of magnitude.

Use the Gaussian for coupling, never for leakage. Coupling is dominated by the bright centre where the fit is excellent. Bend loss and crosstalk are dominated by the faint tails, where the fit is wrong by a factor of a thousand. This matters for the glass bridge because it carries sixteen channels side by side.

Repeat it yourself

  1. Open the mode explorer.
  2. At 1550 nm and NA 0.14, confirm V and the Marcuse MFD against the numbers above.
  3. Drag the wavelength down and find where V crosses 2.405. With NA 0.14 this happens near 1500 nm, which is the contradiction described above.
  4. Slide NA until cutoff lands at 1260 nm. Record it.
  5. Return to 1550 nm and slide NA until the Marcuse MFD reads 10.4 µm. Record it. The two should agree within a few percent.
  6. Compare "power in cladding" at 1310 nm and at 1550 nm, and connect the difference to the bend-loss mechanism above.

Check yourself

Why is the mode wider than the core?

Because the core does not contain the light, it steers it. Guiding is by a 0.45% index difference, not a wall, and a substantial fraction of the power travels in the cladding. If a calculation gives w/a below 1, it is wrong.

What does the number 2.405 actually come from?

It is the first zero of the Bessel function J₀. Solving Maxwell's equations in a cylinder gives Bessel functions in the core and modified Bessel functions in the cladding, and that root is where the second mode first becomes guided.

The same fiber is used at 1310 nm and 1550 nm. Which has the larger mode, and which bends worse?

1550 nm on both counts, and for the same reason. V has wavelength in the denominator, so the longer wavelength gives a smaller V, a mode held less tightly, more power in the cladding, and more to lose when the fiber curves.

Two fibers have identical core diameters but different mode field diameters. Will they splice well?

No. Coupling is governed by the overlap of the two mode fields, not by the geometry of their cores. MFD is the quantity that matters, which is why it is the number that leaves this stage.

Why can Zemax not be used to model this stage?

Ray optics requires apertures large compared with the wavelength. A single-mode fiber has one mode and no rays. The applicable tools are mode solvers and overlap integrals, or the analytic Gaussian approximation.

What stage 1 hands to stage 2

Two things, and they are the entire interface:

  1. A mode of 10.4 µm MFD arrives at the polished glass edge, measured the way the datasheet measures it. The waveguide in the glass has to match that, and matching means overlap of fields, not equality of core sizes.
  2. That mode has exponential tails, not Gaussian ones. Use the Gaussian for the coupling calculation, never for estimating what leaks into the neighbouring channel.
