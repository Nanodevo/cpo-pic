Stage 4: inside the chip

Once the light is in the silicon, the chip is a circuit. How a whole die is simulated as a chain of scattering matrices; the ring resonator, the component that sorts wavelengths, with every formula its design turns on and the numbers of this die's own rings; and the difference between the ring that measures and the ring that demultiplexes. This stage is in progress: the switch cell, the cutback spirals and the add-drop redesign follow.

From fields to circuits

Stages 1 to 3 solved Maxwell's equations on a cross-section, or along a few hundred micrometres of taper, and that is as far as a field solver goes. A die is 5 mm on a side with two centimetres of coiled waveguide on it; at 20 nm cells a single layer is sixty billion points, and no computer solves that. The industry does not try. It simulates in two levels. Every component, a straight, a bend, a coupler, a taper, gets a scattering matrix: for each wavelength, the complex amplitude that leaves each port for a unit amplitude entering each other port.

b = S · a (bj = Σi Sji ai)
(1)
a is the vector of incoming wave amplitudes, one per port, b the outgoing ones, and S the scattering matrix. |Sji|² is the power fraction from port i to port j; its phase is the delay. A straight waveguide of length L has a single off-diagonal element, eiβL times an amplitude loss; the taper's S21 is the 74.5% of stage 3. The matrices of stage 3's eigenmode expansion and of a circuit solver are the same object at different scales.

The second level is the circuit solver. It reads the layout's netlist, which component connects to which at which port, takes each component's matrix from a compact model, a formula or a lookup that returns the matrix for the component's parameters, and multiplies them all together along the connections into one matrix for the whole circuit. The one used here is SAX,[22] open source, and it solves the ring bank in under a second. The die exports one port per channel, so the same machinery scales to all twenty-four.

The ring resonator

A ring is a closed loop of waveguide beside a straight one, the bus, with a gap of a few hundred nanometres between them. Light in the bus leaks into the ring through the same evanescent tail that did the hand-off of stage 3, only sideways. What happens next depends on the wavelength alone:

neff(λm) · L = m · λm, m = 1, 2, 3, …
(2)
The resonance condition. L is the round-trip length of the ring, neff the effective index of its waveguide, and m an integer. When the optical path around the loop is a whole number of wavelengths, light returning to the gap is in step with light leaking in, and it builds up round after round; at every other wavelength the returning light is out of step, cancels, and the bus is untouched. The ring of this die is a racetrack: two straights of 4 µm, two of 0.6 µm, and four Euler bends of radius 10 µm, L = 75.75 µm.

Consecutive resonances differ by one in m, so their spacing follows from equation (2), with one subtlety: neff itself changes with wavelength, and the derivative brings in the group index.

FSR = Δλ = λ² / ( ng · L ), ng = neff − λ · dneff/dλ
(3)
The free spectral range, the wavelength spacing between neighbouring dips of one ring, and the group index, the index that governs how the phase of a mode changes with wavelength; for a 500 × 220 nm silicon strip in oxide the mode solver gives neff = 2.482 and ng = 4.063 at 1550 nm, and the group index is what appears here because it is the rate of change of phase that decides where the next resonance falls. With L = 75.75 µm: FSR = 7.81 nm. The circuit solver's spectrum shows 7.79 nm between dips of the same ring. Measured on a real chip, the same equation is read the other way: the FSR gives ng, and ng is the first number a fabricated waveguide is graded by.

Coupling, loss, and how deep a dip is

Two quantities compete inside a ring. Light enters from the bus at each pass; light is lost around the loop to sidewall roughness and absorption. Their ratio decides everything about the dip.

a² = 10−αL/10, ploss = 1 − a²
(4)
a is the round-trip amplitude, the fraction of the field that survives one loop, and α the propagation loss in dB per unit length. At 2.5 dB/cm, a typical value for an etched silicon strip and a placeholder here until a cutback measurement replaces it, the 75.75 µm loop loses 0.0189 dB per round trip: ploss = 0.44%.
K = sin²( κ · leff ), κ(g) = κ0 · e−γ (g − g0), γ = k0 √( neff² − nox² )
(5)
K is the power coupling per pass, the fraction of the bus light that enters the ring each time it passes the gap. κ is the coupling per unit length between the two parallel waveguides, from the splitting of their symmetric and antisymmetric supermodes exactly as in stage 3, equation (4) there: for two 500 nm strips 200 nm apart the mode solver gives κ0 = 0.0385 rad/µm. leff is the length over which ring and bus interact, the racetrack's 4 µm straight plus the approach of its bends, √(2πR/γ) = 2.77 µm, in all 6.77 µm. γ is the decay rate of the mode's field outside the silicon, 8.18 per µm, and it sets how fast the coupling falls as the gap g widens from the solved gap g0 = 200 nm. At 200 nm, the gap of the first version of the die: K = 6.6%. At the 300 nm it draws now: K = 1.3%.

With t = √(1 − K) the fraction of the field that stays in the bus at each pass, the transmission of a single ring on its bus, the all-pass ring, is[20]

T(φ) = ( a² − 2 a t cosφ + t² ) / ( 1 − 2 a t cosφ + a² t² ), φ = 2π neff L / λ
(6)
φ is the round-trip phase; cosφ = 1 at resonance. There the transmission falls to Tmin = ((a − t)/(1 − a t))², and it is zero, an infinitely deep dip, when a = t: the light leaking back into the bus exactly cancels the light still arriving. In power, that is
critical coupling: K = ploss (equivalently t = a)
(7)
The coupling per pass equals the loss per round trip: light enters exactly as fast as the ring can dissipate it. With K > ploss the ring is over-coupled, most of what enters leaks straight back, and the dip is shallow; with K < ploss it is under-coupled and the dip is shallow again. The first version of the die drew 200 nm: over-coupled fifteen-fold, 6.6% against 0.44%, and dips of 1.1 dB. Solving equations (5) and (7) for the gap: sin²θ = 0.0044 gives θ = 0.0663 rad, κ = θ/leff = 0.0098 per µm, and g = g0 + ln(κ0/κ)/γ = 0.20 + 1.37/8.18 = 0.367 µm. The circuit model at a 370 nm gap gives a dip 34 dB deep. The condition first, the solver as confirmation. The die now draws 300 nm, short of that value on purpose: K = 1.3%, three times the assumed loss, and a dip 5.9 dB deep. The loss is a placeholder until a cutback measures it, and a real loss above 2.5 dB/cm moves the ring towards critical coupling, not past it. The die hedges the other way too: channels 14 and 15 carry the same four rings at 367 nm, so the two banks side by side show which gap the real loss favours.
ΔλFWHM = ( FSR / π ) · ( 1 − a t ) / √( a t ), Q = λ / ΔλFWHM
(8)
The width of the dip at half its depth, and the quality factor Q, the resonance wavelength over that width, which is also the number of round trips the light makes before it is used up, times 2π/m. This is what the deep dip costs: at the 200 nm gap the dip is 0.09 nm wide and Q ≈ 17,000; at the 300 nm now drawn, 0.022 nm and Q ≈ 71,000; at critical coupling it is 0.011 nm wide and Q ≈ 140,000. A narrower dip sorts wavelengths more finely, and it also drifts off its channel with a smaller temperature change, about 0.08 nm per kelvin in silicon, which is why every ring in a product carries a heater and a control loop.

The ring that measures and the ring that sorts

In the all-pass ring nothing comes out anywhere. The resonant wavelength is removed from the bus and turned into heat over a few thousand round trips. That makes it a test structure, and it is what channels 9 and 10 are: from its dips one reads the free spectral range, the group index, the loss and the coupling, everything above. It cannot hand a wavelength to a detector. For that the ring needs a second waveguide, the drop bus, on its far side, and it becomes an add-drop ring:

Dres = ( 1 − t1² )( 1 − t2² ) a / ( 1 − a t1 t2 )², matched when t1 = a t2
(9)
Dres is the power reaching the drop port at resonance, with t1 and t2 the bus-side and drop-side through amplitudes of the two couplers. The balance condition of equation (7) becomes: coupling in equals coupling out plus the loss. Both gaps are now knobs, and the ring's own loss, which was the whole point of the all-pass ring, becomes a waste that lowers the dropped power. Eight such rings in a row, each tuned to one channel, sort eight wavelengths into eight drop ports, each ending on a photodetector: the receive side of a wavelength-division-multiplexed transceiver, the circuit the die's ring bank is the skeleton of.

The switch cell

The other circuit element on the die is a Mach-Zehnder interferometer, the element a switch is made of. The splitter divides the light into two equal halves in step; the arms give the two halves a phase difference; the combiner recombines them, and where the light comes out depends only on that difference. For an ideal splitter and a 50:50 combiner:

Pupper = ½ ( 1 − sin Δφ ), Plower = ½ ( 1 + sin Δφ ), Δφ = φheater + 2π neff ΔL / λ
(10)
Δφ is the phase difference between the arms. One part is fixed by the layout, the 100 µm length difference ΔL times the propagation constant; the other is set by the heater. With Δφ = −π/2 everything leaves by the upper output, with +π/2 by the lower, and at zero the light divides equally. A switch toggles between the two ends, a swing of π. This is the routing element of an optical engine: a bit of current chooses which of two paths a signal takes. The engine's switches use a 2 × 2 coupler on both sides, so that two signals can be routed; on the die the input side is a 1 × 2, because only one input is fed, and the combiner is the engine's 2 × 2.
φheater = ( 2π/λ ) · ( dn/dT ) · Lh · ΔT
(11)
The thermo-optic effect: silicon's index rises with temperature at dn/dT = 1.86 × 10−4 per kelvin, so warming a length Lh of the arm by ΔT adds phase. For the 200 µm heater here, a shift of π needs ΔT = λ / (2 Lh dn/dT) = 21 K. How many milliwatts that takes depends on the heater's geometry and the heat path to the substrate, a thermal problem this study has not solved; tens of milliwatts is the usual figure for this kind of heater, and it is the number that makes large switch fabrics power-hungry. On this die the heater has no pads, so the phase is read, not set: from the wavelength, below.

Three things the picture teaches. The two outputs add to a constant at each wavelength: a lossless interferometer never destroys light, it only steers it. That is why both outputs are brought to the glass: their ratio, Pupper / (Pupper + Plower), divides out everything the two paths share, the coupling to the glass, the arms' loss and the hump, and leaves the interference alone; in the model it swings from 0 to 1 at every fringe. Second, the depth of the fringes in that ratio reads how evenly the combiner splits, equation (13). And third, the wavelength dependence with the heater off is not a defect; the imbalance is chosen so that a passive sweep shows sixteen fringes and the cell can be graded with no current at all. A balanced cell, ΔL = 0, would be flat in wavelength and would need the heater on to be characterised. The first version of the die drew a 10 µm imbalance, about 55 nm per fringe: fewer than two fringes in a sweep, too few to fit.

The interferometer from first principles

Everything the switch does follows from one fact: light is a wave, and two waves that meet add as waves, not as powers. Two equal waves in step give twice the amplitude and four times the power; half a wave apart they give nothing, and the power has not vanished, it has gone out of the other port. The interferometer is a machine for setting that phase difference and reading the result. Its two couplers are described by one matrix each:

C = [ t iκ ] [ iκ t ], t = √(1 − K), κ = √K
(12)
The coupler matrix. K is the fraction of the power that crosses from one waveguide to the other, one half for a 50:50 coupler. The crossed wave picks up a quarter-wave phase, the factor i, which is not a convention but a consequence of energy conservation: without it the two outputs could add up to more than the input. The matrix takes the two incoming amplitudes to the two outgoing ones. Between the couplers the arms are a diagonal matrix of phases, eiφ1 and eiφ2, and an interferometer with a coupler on both sides is the product C · D · C. The die's cell has a 1 × 2 splitter in front instead, which hands both arms the amplitude 1/√2 in step, so the light comes out as
bupper = ( t eiφ1 + i κ eiφ2 ) / √2, blower = ( i κ eiφ1 + t eiφ2 ) / √2
(13a)
Pupper, lower = ½ [ 1 ∓ 2 √( K (1 − K) ) sin Δφ ]
(13)
On the die the first element is a 1 × 2 splitter, so the arms start with equal amplitudes 1/√2 in step, and only the combiner is the matrix C above. Δφ = φ1 − φ2. Each output is a mix of both arms, with the crossed one a quarter wave behind; that quarter wave is why the equal split sits at Δφ = 0 and the full ones at ±π/2. For K = ½ these are equation (10). The two always add to 1: nothing is lost, only steered. An imperfect combiner shrinks both swings alike: the factor 2√(K(1 − K)) is the visibility, 0.98 for a 40:60 combiner, so each output's null bottoms at ½(1 − 0.98) = 1%, a 20 dB extinction ratio. That factor is exactly what the fringe depth of the ratio above measures. An imbalanced splitter adds a second, similar loss of visibility; one sweep cannot tell the two apart, which is the price of a single input.
Δφ = ( 2π/λ ) [ neff ΔL + Δn · Lh ]
(14)
Where the phase difference comes from. A length difference ΔL between the arms, fixed by the layout: the 100 µm of this cell, which makes the outputs sweep through a full cycle every λ²/(ngΔL) of wavelength, the 5.9 nm of the spectrum above. Or an index difference Δn over a length Lh, imposed from outside: by heat, equation (11), slow but strong; by an electric field through carriers in a diode, fast but weak, which is how a Mach-Zehnder modulator writes data onto light at tens of gigabits per second; or by a change in whatever touches the waveguide, which is how the same structure becomes a sensor. The interferometer does not care what caused Δφ; it only reads it.
dP/dΔφ is largest where the outputs are equal: the quadrature point
(15)
For a switch the useful points are the two ends, all the light in one output or the other. For a modulator or a sensor the useful point is halfway, where the output changes fastest and most linearly with the phase: the interferometer is biased there, usually with a heater, and the signal rides on the slope. With a 1 × 2 splitter in front, the halfway point is Δφ = 0, the balanced arms; with a coupler on both sides it is Δφ = π/2. The same cell, three different jobs, chosen by where on the curve it is parked.

What stage 4 has established so far

  1. A chip is simulated in two levels: field solvers for components, a circuit solver for the whole, joined by each component's scattering matrix.
  2. A ring resonates when its round trip holds a whole number of wavelengths; the spacing of its resonances is λ²/(ngL), and the group index, not the effective index, is what sets it.
  3. The depth of a dip is decided by two numbers, the coupling per pass and the loss per round trip. They are equal at critical coupling, and the gap is the knob: 367 nm for this ring at the assumed loss. The die draws 300 nm, short of it, because the loss is only assumed, and a second bank at 367 nm beside it.
  4. The deep dip costs linewidth: Q rises from 17,000 to 140,000, and with it the sensitivity to temperature. Heaters follow.
  5. The die's rings are all-pass: they measure. Sorting wavelengths needs add-drop rings with a second bus, and that redesign is the next step of this stage.
  6. A Mach-Zehnder interferometer steers light by a phase difference between two arms; the heater supplies the phase through the thermo-optic effect, 21 K for π over 200 µm. On the die its 100 µm imbalance gives about sixteen fringes per sweep, enough to grade the cell with no current.
  7. Both outputs reach the glass. They always add up to the same total, so their ratio removes the coupling loss and leaves the interference; its fringe depth reads the combiner's balance through the visibility 2√(K(1 − K)). Parked at its equal split the same cell is a modulator or a sensor.

Check yourself

A measured ring shows dips 6.2 nm apart, and its round trip is 95 µm. What is the group index of the waveguide?

From equation (3), ng = λ²/(FSR · L) = (1.55)²/(0.0062 × 95) = 4.08. This is how a fabricated waveguide's group index is measured: from a ring, not from a mode solver.

The dips of a ring are shallow. Is the ring over- or under-coupled?

Either; a shallow dip alone does not say. The width tells them apart: an over-coupled ring has a broad dip, an under-coupled one a narrow dip. Both are far from t = a.

Why do the four rings of the die have radii 24 nm apart?

So that their resonances fall at different wavelengths, evenly spread. Each step shifts a ring's dips by about λ · (ΔL/L) · (neff/ng), here 2.0 nm, a quarter of the 7.8 nm free spectral range, so the four sets interleave evenly. The first version of the die stepped 50 nm, a shift of about half the free spectral range: ring 3 then fell within 0.5 nm of ring 1, and ring 4 within 0.5 nm of ring 2. In a demultiplexer each ring would sit on its own channel.

The cell's outputs peak at 85% each in the model, and at every wavelength the two add up to the same 85%. Where is the other 15%?

In the model's placeholder losses: 0.3 dB assumed for each MMI and the propagation loss of the arms. The interferometer itself steers all the light; that is why the ratio of the two outputs swings from 0 to 1 while each output on its own does not.

You need a deep dip and a wide one at the same time. Can the gap give you both?

No. The gap sets the coupling, and at fixed loss the coupling sets both the depth and the width through the same product a t. A wide, deep dip needs more loss, or a different structure such as a coupled pair of rings.
