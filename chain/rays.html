Foundations: rays, lenses and aberration

Where light can be treated as rays, what a lens actually does to them, and the first failure that makes optical design a profession rather than an equation. Every result below was measured in a browser, and every exercise can be repeated in a few minutes.

Ray optics is an approximation, and knowing exactly where it stops being true is more useful than knowing where it works. It holds when every aperture in the system is large compared with the wavelength. It fails completely for a single-mode waveguide, which is why nothing from this page can be used on stages 1 to 4 of the chain. It is included because the language of optical design (focal length, aperture, aberration, spot size) was built here, and because the moment a design puts light into free space, this is the physics that governs it.

The tool used throughout is the free browser sandbox Ray Optics Simulation. No account, no install. Turn on Grid and Snap to Grid under the settings gear before starting, so distances can be counted instead of estimated.

1. Focal length, found rather than looked up

Place a point source and an ideal lens, then slide the source along the axis until the rays leaving the lens are parallel. The distance at which that happens is the focal length, and the point the source sits on is the front focal point. Run it backwards and the same statement reads: a collimated beam entering the lens comes to a focus at f.

The two sides of a lens are not independent. Move the source further out and the image moves closer in. Take that to its limit: a source infinitely far away images at exactly f. That limit is the definition of focal length.

The lens equation, by measurement

With an ideal lens of f = 100 and the source placed at 200, the image forms at 200 on the other side. Object and image distances are equal, and the image is the same size as the object. This is the only configuration where that happens.

1/a + 1/b = 1/f    1/200 + 1/200 = 1/100  ✓

The 2f to 2f case is worth committing to memory as a sanity check. If a relay you have built produces an image the same size as its object, it is at 2f to 2f whether or not that was the intention.

2. Thick lenses have no single origin

As soon as a lens has real thickness, the question "measured from where?" has no clean answer. Three distances exist and they are not equal:

QuantityMeasured fromTo
Effective focal length, EFLthe principal plane, an abstraction inside the glassthe focus
Back focal distance, BFDthe last glass surfacethe focus
Front focal distance, FFDthe front focal pointthe first glass surface
BFD = f · [ 1 − (n−1)·d / (n·R1) ] FFD = f · [ 1 − (n−1)·d / (n·R2) ]

For a plano-convex lens with R1 = 100, R2 = ∞, d = 20 and n = 1.5, giving f = 200, those evaluate to BFD = 186.67 and FFD = 200. The simulator's own readout gives 186.66 and 200.

Turn the same lens around and the two swap. That is why the measured focus position moves by 15 units when a lens is flipped, even though its power has not changed by a hair. A catalogue that quotes EFL where you assumed BFD will cost you the difference in your mechanical stack, and it is a routine way to lose several millimetres on an assembly.

3. Real surfaces do not have one focus

An ideal lens obeys 1/a + 1/b = 1/f for every ray at every height. It is a mathematical object. Replace it with a real spherical lens, send in a collimated beam, and the rays no longer cross at a single point: rays through the edge of the lens cross closer to it than rays near the axis.

The cause is one substitution. Snell's law contains sin θ. Paraxial optics, the entire 1/a + 1/b = 1/f framework, is what you get by replacing sin θ with θ. That is the first term of a series, and spherical aberration is the next one. A spherical surface is not the correct shape for imaging; it is the shape that is easy to grind and easy to test.

Longitudinal spherical aberration

LSA(h) = z(paraxial ray) − z(ray at height h)

Measured along the axis, in units of length, from any convenient origin: since it is a difference of two positions, whichever origin you choose cancels. To lowest order it grows as the square of ray height,

LSA ∝ h²

which is why fast lenses are hard and why stopping a lens down cleans it up so dramatically. Halve the aperture and the aberration falls by four.

There is no single best focus. The tightest blur, the circle of least confusion, sits roughly three quarters of the way from the paraxial focus toward the marginal focus. For the measurements below, with paraxial at 195 and marginal at 185, best focus is near 187, not 195.

4. Which way round a lens faces

The same plano-convex lens, same focal length, tested in both orientations. Collimated beam in, marginal ray at h = 40, paraxial ray at h = 10, lens 100 tall, d = 20, n = 1.5.

OrientationR1R2paraxialmarginalLSA
curved side to beam100∞19518510
flat side to beam∞−10021017040

The reason is visible in the drawing. With the flat side facing the beam, the rays arrive perpendicular to that surface, and a surface at normal incidence bends nothing. The entire deflection then has to happen at the single curved back surface. With the curved side facing the beam, the front surface does part of the work and the flat back surface finishes it, because by then the rays are no longer perpendicular to it.

Aberration at a surface grows faster than the deflection that surface produces. Two surfaces each bending a little beat one surface bending a lot. Hence the working rule: point the curved side at the collimated space.

5. Bending: being the optimiser by hand

If sharing the work between two surfaces beats loading one, why stop at a flat back surface? Curve both and share it further. Hold the focal length fixed and the only free parameter left is the shape, or bending.

Because the lens is thick, the thin-lens relation is not accurate enough to hold f constant across the set. The exact version is

1/f = (n−1) · [ 1/R1 − 1/R2 + (n−1)·d / (n·R1·R2) ]

The radii below all give exactly f = 200 with d = 20 and n = 1.5, so the only thing changing between rows is the bending. This is a one-factor, six-level experiment whose response is the blur.

Expect the bottom of the curve to be flat. Shapes 1 to 3 should land close together while 5 and 6 are dramatically worse. If two of them read the same on the ruler, that is the finding rather than a failed measurement: near an optimum the response surface is flat, and measurement resolution decides whether the minimum can be located at all. To force the differences open, push the marginal ray to h = 48, which raises every LSA by about 1.4× and leaves the ranking untouched.

Doing this by hand is worth more than the answer it produces. A Zemax optimisation run varies the radii, holds the focal length as a constraint and minimises a merit function built from ray errors. It is faster. It is not doing anything different.

Repeat it yourself

  1. Open the simulator, turn on Grid and Snap to Grid.
  2. Light Source → Beam, dragged vertically on the left so it throws a wide bundle of parallel rays to the right.
  3. Glass → Spherical Lens, dragged 100 tall. Set R1, R2, d and n from the table.
  4. Light Source → Single ray twice, at h = 40 and h = 10 above the axis, aimed parallel to it.
  5. Other → Ruler along the axis. Read where each ray crosses. Subtract.
If the simulator rejects the lens with "invalid lens parameters", it is geometry, not a bug. The sag of a curved surface is sag = R − √(R² − h²) with h the semi-diameter, and the centre thickness d must exceed it, or the rim would have negative thickness. Minimum edge thickness is a real manufacturability constraint, and in Zemax it is a boundary condition placed on the optimiser for exactly this reason.

Check yourself

A collimated beam enters a lens. Where does it focus, and does moving the lens change that?

At the focal length behind the lens, and no. In collimated space there is no length that matters, which is why an expanded-beam connector can tolerate a gap that a butt joint cannot.

Why does flipping a plano-convex lens change the measured focus position by 15 units when its focal length has not changed?

Because BFD and FFD swap when the lens is reversed. The focal length is measured from the principal planes, which move; the ruler is placed at the glass surface, which does not.

You halve the aperture of a lens. What happens to the longitudinal spherical aberration?

It falls to a quarter. LSA grows as h². This is what stopping down buys, and the price is a quarter of the light.

Why is spherical aberration the first aberration a designer attacks?

Because it is the only one that survives on the axis. Coma, astigmatism, field curvature and distortion all vanish at the centre of the field and grow off it. Spherical aberration is at full strength in the middle of the image.
