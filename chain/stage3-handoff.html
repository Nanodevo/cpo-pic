Stage 3: the hand-off from glass to silicon

How light leaves a 6 µm glass guide and ends up in a 0.5 µm silicon wire without ever crossing an interface. Phase matching, supermodes, the beat length, why the taper as first drawn does not work, the profile that does, and the two rungs of simulation that say so.

The scene

Stage 2 ended with a number that closes the obvious route: butt-coupling the glass guide's 6.6 µm mode straight into a silicon wire's 0.5 µm mode would cost 5.55 dB in size mismatch alone. The hand-off is therefore not a joint at all. The two guides lie side by side, one on each side of a 1 µm adhesive layer, and the light moves from one to the other through their overlapping evanescent tails while both guides run in parallel for two millimetres.[6] Nothing is cut, nothing is aligned face to face, and the transfer is decided by the shape of the silicon along the way.

The parts, with the numbers this stage uses throughout. The glass guide is the 6 × 4 µm ion-exchanged channel of stage 2 with an index step Δn = 0.012, whose fundamental mode has an effective index neff = 1.50522 and a mode field diameter of 6.6 µm. The adhesive is 1 µm of index-matched glue, n = 1.500. The silicon is a 220 nm thick strip whose width w changes along the light: 130 nm at the tip, 500 nm at the far end, over a length L = 2 mm. Effective index is the one symbol the whole stage turns on:

β = k0 · neff, k0 = 2π/λ
(1)
β is the propagation constant of a guided mode, the phase it accumulates per micrometre along the guide, in radians per micrometre. k0 is the free-space wavenumber at the wavelength λ = 1.55 µm, so k0 = 4.054 rad/µm. neff is the index a plane wave would need in order to travel at the mode's phase velocity. A mode in a waveguide has one neff, and it lies between the cladding index and the core index.

Phase matching: where the two guides agree

A wide silicon strip guides tightly: at w = 500 nm the mode has neff = 2.450, most of its power inside the silicon. Narrow the strip and the mode spills out. Its neff falls toward the index of whatever surrounds it, which here is the adhesive at 1.500. The table is the finite-element solution of the isolated silicon strip at 1550 nm, transverse-electric polarization, the electric field along the width.[10]

Two things to read off. First, below about 141 nm the isolated strip is not a waveguide at all: its would-be neff drops under 1.500 and the mode is no longer bound. A 130 nm tip on its own guides nothing. It exists only as a small perturbation on the glass mode, which is exactly the role it needs to play at the start. Second, on the way down neff passes through the glass guide's value:

neff,Si(w*) = neff,glass = 1.50522 → w* = 156 nm
(2)
This is the phase-matching condition. At the width w* the two guides, each solved alone, carry modes with the same propagation constant. Only there can power move between them freely, because only there do the two fields stay in step as they travel. Everywhere else they drift apart in phase and the exchange averages out.

Where w* sits depends on the glass, not on the silicon. The silicon curve is fixed by the strip thickness and the platform; the glass line is set by how much ion exchange the bridge maker did. The finite-element solve repeated over a range of glass contrasts:

This is why the taper sweeps from 130 nm all the way to 500 nm rather than from 150 to 160: it must pass through the crossing wherever the glass process actually put it. It is also why the test die carries tapers with three different tip widths, 120, 150 and 180 nm, on channels 11 to 14. A tip that starts at 180 nm begins on the wide side of every crossing in the table, and the light launched from the glass would find the silicon already out of step.

Solved together: supermodes and the anticrossing

Now solve the two guides as one structure. The modes of the pair are not the glass mode and the silicon mode; they are two supermodes, each living partly in both. Coupled-mode theory[14] describes this with two amplitudes: A(z) for the field in the silicon mode and B(z) for the field in the glass mode, both slowly varying along z, with the glass mode's phase factored out so that only the difference between the guides remains:

dA/dz = −i · [ k0δ(z) · A + κ · B ] dB/dz = −i · κ · A
(3)
δ(z) = neff,Si(w(z)) − neff,glass is the detuning, in index units: how far apart the two isolated guides are at the width the taper has reached at z. It is negative on the narrow side of the crossing and positive on the wide side. κ is the coupling coefficient in radians per micrometre, set by how much the two mode fields overlap across the adhesive. Thicker adhesive, smaller κ. i is the imaginary unit; the minus-i form means each amplitude advances in phase as it propagates.

With δ held fixed, equation (3) has two solutions that propagate unchanged, the supermodes, and their effective indices are

n± = neff,glass + δ/2 ± ½ √( δ² + G² ), G = 2κ/k0
(4)
G is the gap: the smallest separation the two supermodes ever reach, in index units, attained exactly at the crossing where δ = 0. Away from the crossing the curves approach the isolated ones, n+ tending to whichever isolated index is larger. The full two-guide finite-element solve at the crossing gives G = 2.8 × 10−3 for this geometry, and that single number, together with the isolated silicon curve, is all the two-mode model needs.

How much of the upper supermode lives in the silicon follows from the same algebra:

fSi = ½ · [ 1 + δ / √( δ² + G² ) ]
(5)
fSi is the fraction of the upper supermode's power carried in the silicon. Far on the narrow side (δ << −G) it is 0: the mode is the glass mode with a whisper of silicon. At the crossing it is exactly ½. Far on the wide side it is 1. The full solve agrees: at the 120, 150 and 180 nm tips the upper supermode is 95%, 72% and 18% in the glass respectively, and half the power has crossed at about 158 nm.

That is the whole mechanism in one sentence: if the taper changes slowly enough, the light stays in the upper supermode, and the upper supermode turns from glass into silicon by itself. Nothing is coupled "across" in the ordinary sense. The mode the light is in simply changes its shape as the structure changes underneath it. Everything that follows is about the words "slowly enough".

The beat length

Launch light into the glass mode alone at some fixed width. The glass mode is not a supermode; it is an equal mixture of the two, and the two travel at different speeds. As they slip in phase the power sloshes from one guide to the other and back. One full slosh takes

Lb = λ / ( n+ − n− ) = λ / √( δ² + G² )
(6)
Lb is the beat length, the distance over which the two supermodes accumulate one full wave of phase difference. At the crossing it is longest, λ/G = 1.55 µm / 0.0028 ≈ 550 µm; the two-guide solve gives 556 µm. Full transfer at fixed geometry takes half of that, 278 µm. Away from the crossing Lb shortens but the slosh is incomplete: at 180 nm it is 109 µm long and only about 18% of the power ever visits the other guide.

A number to hold on to: 550 µm is half a millimetre, at a place where the guides are a micrometre apart. The two lengths are not in competition. The adhesive thickness sets κ, κ sets G, and G sets Lb. A thicker bondline makes the slosh slower, and it is the slowness that the taper must respect.

Offered against needed

The taper as first drawn on the die is linear: 120 nm at the tip, 500 nm at 2 mm, so it widens at 0.19 nm per micrometre. The hand-off happens where the supermode changes character, roughly between 140 and 180 nm, and the linear taper crosses that 40 nm of width in 210 µm. Along the same stretch, the beat length from equation (6) rises from 109 µm at 180 nm through 550 µm at the crossing and falls back to 250 µm at 140 nm. The taper leaves the crossing region in less than half a slosh.

A correction is recorded here rather than hidden. An earlier version of this figure took its beat lengths from the two-guide solver's "splitting" column, which reported up to 1.8 mm on the narrow side. That column had, away from the crossing, measured the distance to the wrong neighbouring mode, one of the glass guide's own higher modes rather than the silicon-like supermode. Equation (6), which is the same two-mode relation the propagation model integrates, is what the figure now shows. The conclusion is unchanged; the numbers on the narrow side are smaller.

Adiabaticity, made a number

"Slowly enough" can be quantified. Write the upper supermode's composition as a mixing angle θ, with fSi = sin²θ, so that θ runs from 0 on the glass side to π/2 on the silicon side. The light stays in the supermode if the angle turns slowly compared with the rate at which the two supermodes drift apart in phase. The ratio is the adiabaticity parameter:[16], [17]

ε(z) = | dθ/dz | / ( k0 √( δ² + G² ) ) = G · | dδ/dz | / ( 2 k0 ( δ² + G² )3/2 )
(7)
The denominator is the local beat rate in radians per micrometre, the phase the supermodes gain on each other per unit length. The numerator is how fast the mode's composition is being asked to change. ε << 1 means the structure changes over many beat lengths and the transfer is adiabatic; ε near 1 means the structure changes within one beat and the light cannot follow. The second form uses tan 2θ = G/δ. The (δ² + G²)3/2 in the denominator is the point: at the crossing δ = 0 and ε is largest, so it is there that the taper must be slowest.

For the linear taper, dδ/dz is the slope of the silicon curve at the crossing, about 4.5 × 10−4 per nanometre of width, times the taper's 0.19 nm per micrometre, giving 8.5 × 10−5 per micrometre. Put into equation (7) at δ = 0, ε comes out at 1.4. The linear taper is not adiabatic where it matters; it is, at the crossing, about as far from adiabatic as a taper can be.

The repair follows from asking the opposite question: what profile holds ε constant? Set ε to a chosen value in equation (7) and integrate:[17]

z(δ) = δ / ( 2 ε k0 G √( δ² + G² ) ) + constant
(8)
This gives position as a function of detuning, and through the silicon curve, as a function of width. The profile crawls through the crossing, where the bracket is smallest, and races through the wide part, where nothing is happening. Choosing ε so that the whole profile is 2 mm long gives ε = 0.042. Read back onto the layout: the shaped taper spends 1.78 of its 2 mm between 140 and 162 nm of width and does all of 162 to 500 nm in the last 240 µm. The linear taper spends 210 µm in the same window.

There is a classical result for what happens when a linear sweep passes a crossing too fast. Landau and Zener solved exactly this two-level problem in 1932, for an atom whose energy levels are swept through a crossing in time;[15] replace time with z and energy with propagation constant and the mathematics is identical. The probability that the light does not follow the supermode, and stays in the glass, is

Pstay = exp( −2π κ² / | d(k0δ)/dz | )
(9)
κ = k0G/2 is the coupling coefficient from equation (4), and the denominator is the rate at which the detuning is swept, in radians per micrometre squared. For the linear taper the exponent is about 0.55, so Pstay ≈ 0.58: the Landau-Zener estimate says 58% of the light is left behind in the glass and at most 42% reaches the silicon. The formula assumes the sweep runs from far below the crossing to far above it, which a taper starting at 130 nm does not quite do; that is what the next section tests.

Rung 1: integrating the two-mode equations

Equation (3) is two coupled ordinary differential equations with a z-dependent coefficient, and a numerical integrator solves them in a fraction of a second for any profile. All the light starts in the glass, B(0) = 1, A(0) = 0, and the quantity reported is |A(L)|², the power in the silicon at the end.

Three readings. The linear taper does better than Landau-Zener predicts, 62% against 43%, because it does not start far from the crossing: at 130 nm the detuning is only 2.2 gaps below it, so the sweep is truncated and part of the "lost" light was never given the chance to be lost. The shaped taper reaches 94% at the same length, and the bottom panel shows why the number is not higher and why it is not monotonic in length: the profile begins abruptly at that same tip, 2.2 gaps from the crossing, where the glass mode is already 5% silicon. That mismatch launches a few percent into the lower supermode, which then beats against the upper one, and the final fraction oscillates with length around a level the abrupt start has set. A 314 µm shaped taper happening to land on a crest at 99.1% is the oscillation, not a better design. The cure is a gentler start, a tip below 130 nm or a lead-in that eases the silicon in, and 130 nm is already at the edge of what lithography draws.

What the two-mode model cannot see: radiation into the adhesive, the transverse-magnetic mode, the glass guide's own higher modes, reflection, and the fact that κ itself changes along the taper as the silicon mode swells. It is a model of one mechanism, and it says the mechanism is marginal for the linear taper and comfortable for the shaped one. Whether the other mechanisms take a share is what rung 2 is for.

Rung 2: eigenmode expansion in three dimensions

Eigenmode expansion (EME) is the method built for structures like this one: long along the light, slowly varying, with nothing happening in the transverse plane that a mode solver cannot capture.[18] The taper is cut into cells along z. In each cell the cross-section is frozen and its modes are solved, all of them, glass-like, silicon-like, the transverse-magnetic ones, the leaky ones, up to a chosen count. Within a cell every field is a sum of those modes, each propagating with its own β:

E(x, y, z) = Σm [ am+ e−iβmz + am− e+iβmz ] · Em(x, y)
(10)
Em(x, y) is the field pattern of mode m in that cell and βm its propagation constant. am+ and am− are the amplitudes travelling forward and backward. At every boundary between cells the fields on the two sides are matched by overlap integrals, which gives a scattering matrix for that interface; the matrices of all cells and interfaces are multiplied together into one scattering matrix for the whole taper. Its element S21 between the glass mode at port 1 and the silicon mode at port 2 is the answer, and |S21|² is the transferred power. Because each cell's modes are solved once, changing the length of every cell by the same factor costs nothing new: a sweep over taper lengths comes free with a single run.

The model as built: a 16 × 12 µm box in the cross-section, the glass and its guide, the adhesive, the silicon strip as a polygon whose width follows the profile, oxide above; a mesh of 20 nm on the silicon and 120 nm on the glass guide, 238 × 162 cells across; 120 slices along the 2.02 mm; eight modes per slice; absorbing layers on the box walls so that radiated light leaves rather than reflects; one wavelength, 1550 nm.[19] The modes at the two ports were cross-checked against the stage-1 and stage-2 solver: the glass mode at port 1 comes out at neff = 1.50520 against 1.50522, the silicon mode at port 2 within 1%. Two jobs were built, the shaped taper with a sweep over seven lengths from 0.3 to 5 mm, and the linear taper with a field monitor down the middle for the picture of the light crossing the bondline. The cloud solver priced the shaped job at 1.33 credits, and it was run on 2026-09-04.

What stage 3 establishes

The stage in ten statements. If any of them is not obvious, that is the section to reread.

  1. The hand-off is not a joint. The guides run in parallel across a 1 µm bondline and the light moves through the overlap of their evanescent tails.
  2. Power moves freely only where the two isolated guides are phase matched, here at a silicon width of 156 nm, and that width is set by the glass, not by the silicon.
  3. Below about 141 nm the isolated silicon strip is not a waveguide at all, which is exactly what a tip should be.
  4. Solved together, the pair has two supermodes that avoid each other by a gap G at the crossing. The light rides the upper one from glass into silicon.
  5. The beat length λ/√(δ²+G²) is the distance of one full exchange. It is longest at the crossing, about 550 µm here, and it is set by the bondline, not compared with it.
  6. Whether the light stays in the supermode is decided by the adiabaticity parameter ε, the rate of change of the mode's composition over the local beat rate.
  7. The linear taper as drawn reaches ε = 1.4 at the crossing. It is not adiabatic where adiabaticity matters.
  8. Holding ε constant gives a profile in closed form. At the same 2 mm it holds ε = 0.042 and spends 1.78 mm inside 22 nm of width.
  9. The two-mode integration says 62% for the linear taper and 94% for the shaped one at 2 mm, with the residual set by the abrupt start at the tip.
  10. A model that tracks one mechanism can only say that mechanism is fine. The three-dimensional solve is the check on everything it left out.

Check yourself

Why is butt-coupling the glass guide to the silicon wire not an option?

Because the modes are 6.6 µm and 0.5 µm across, and coupling is an overlap of fields. The size mismatch alone costs 5.55 dB, before any misalignment. The evanescent hand-off avoids ever putting the two modes face to face.

The bridge maker changes the ion exchange and the glass contrast rises from 0.012 to 0.016. What happens to the taper?

The crossing moves from 156 to 162 nm of silicon width. A taper that sweeps through both is unaffected in principle; a taper designed to start at 160 nm would now start on the wrong side of the crossing and launch most of the light out of step.

The beat length at the crossing is 550 µm. Does the adhesive have to be thinner than that?

No. The two lengths lie along different directions and are not in competition. The adhesive thickness sets the coupling κ, which sets the gap G, which sets the beat length. A thicker bondline lengthens the beat and makes the taper's job slower, it does not compete with the beat length.

Why must the taper be slowest exactly at the crossing?

Because the adiabaticity parameter carries (δ²+G²)3/2 in its denominator. At the crossing δ = 0, the supermodes are closest, the beat is slowest, and any change in the structure is a larger fraction of a beat. The same width change that is harmless at 300 nm is violent at 156 nm.

The linear taper transfers 62% in the two-mode model, but Landau-Zener says 43%. Which is wrong?

Neither. Landau-Zener assumes a sweep from far below the crossing to far above it. The taper starts only 2.2 gaps below the crossing, so the sweep is truncated, and the integrated model, which knows the actual start, gives the higher number. Where the assumptions differ, the estimate is the one to distrust.

A shaped taper of 314 µm transfers 99.1% and one of 2 mm only 93.9%. Should the die use the short one?

No. The transfer oscillates with length because the abrupt start at the tip launches a few percent into the lower supermode, which then beats against the upper one. The short taper happens to land on a crest. A design should not depend on landing on a crest; the fix is a gentler start.

Why does a length sweep in eigenmode expansion cost almost nothing extra?

Because the expensive step is solving the modes of each cell's cross-section, and scaling every cell's length by the same factor changes only the phase each mode accumulates, not the modes themselves. The cell scattering matrices are rebuilt from the same modes and multiplied again.
