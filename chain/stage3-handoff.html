Stage 3: the hand-off from glass to silicon

How light leaves a 6 µm glass guide and ends up in a 0.5 µm silicon wire without ever crossing an interface. Phase matching, supermodes, the beat length, the adiabaticity parameter, the profile that holds it constant, and the three-dimensional check that caught the first design following the wrong polarization.

The scene

Stage 2 ended with a number that closes the obvious route: butt-coupling the glass guide's 6.6 µm mode straight into a silicon wire's 0.5 µm mode would cost 5.55 dB in size mismatch alone. The hand-off is therefore not a joint at all. The two guides lie side by side, one on each side of a 1 µm adhesive layer, and the light moves from one to the other through their overlapping evanescent tails while both guides run in parallel for two millimetres.[6] Nothing is cut, nothing is aligned face to face, and the transfer is decided by the shape of the silicon along the way.

The parts, with the numbers this stage uses throughout. The glass guide is the 6 × 4 µm ion-exchanged channel of stage 2 with an index step Δn = 0.012, whose fundamental mode has an effective index neff = 1.5049 and a mode field diameter of 6.6 µm. The adhesive is 1 µm of index-matched glue, n = 1.500. The silicon is a 220 nm thick strip whose width w changes along the light, from a narrow tip to 500 nm over a length L = 2 mm. Effective index is the one symbol the whole stage turns on:

β = k0 · neff, k0 = 2π/λ
(1)
β is the propagation constant of a guided mode, the phase it accumulates per micrometre along the guide, in radians per micrometre. k0 is the free-space wavenumber at the wavelength λ = 1.55 µm, so k0 = 4.054 rad/µm. neff is the index a plane wave would need in order to travel at the mode's phase velocity. A mode in a waveguide has one neff, and it lies between the cladding index and the core index.

A strip narrower than it is tall has two kinds of mode

A wide silicon strip guides tightly: at w = 500 nm the mode has neff = 2.44, most of its power inside the silicon. Narrow the strip and the mode spills out, and its neff falls toward the index of whatever surrounds it. So far this is the same story as the fiber in stage 1. What is new is that the strip has two fundamental modes, not one, and which of them is "the" fundamental depends on the width.

The two modes are distinguished by the direction of their electric field. One has its field along the width of the strip, horizontal in every figure here; by convention it is called transverse electric, TE, or Ex. The other has its field along the height, vertical, and is called transverse magnetic, TM, or Ey. A mode's field prefers to lie along the larger dimension of the core, because there it can spread with the least penalty. For a strip 500 nm wide and 220 nm tall the horizontal mode is the better confined one and has the higher neff. At 220 nm the strip is square and the two are degenerate. Below 220 nm the strip is taller than it is wide, the vertical mode takes over as the fundamental, and the horizontal one falls away faster.

The glass guide's fundamental mode is horizontally polarized. It can only exchange power with a silicon mode of the same polarization, so the branch that matters for the hand-off is the horizontal, Ex, one. Two things to read off. First, below about 199 nm the isolated strip carries no horizontally polarized mode at all; its would-be neff drops under 1.500 and the field is no longer bound. A 180 nm tip on its own guides nothing in this polarization, which is exactly the role a tip should play: a small perturbation on the glass mode. Second, on the way down neff passes through the glass guide's value:

neff,Si(w*) = neff,glass = 1.5049 → w* = 203 nm
(2)
This is the phase-matching condition. At the width w* the two guides, each solved alone, carry modes with the same propagation constant. Only there can power move between them freely, because only there do the two fields stay in step as they travel. Everywhere else they drift apart in phase and the exchange averages out.

Where w* sits depends on the glass, not on the silicon. The silicon curve is fixed by the strip thickness and the platform; the glass line is set by how much ion exchange the bridge maker did. On the dashed branch, a sweep of glass contrasts from 0.008 to 0.020 moved the crossing by 19 nm; the same sensitivity exists on the solid branch and has not been recomputed. This is why the taper must sweep well past the crossing rather than stop near it, and why the test die carries tapers with three tip widths, 120, 150 and 180 nm, on channels 11 to 14. On the solid branch the 180 nm tip, channels 13 and 14, is the one that starts below the crossing.

Solved together: supermodes and the anticrossing

Now solve the two guides as one structure. The modes of the pair are not the glass mode and the silicon mode; they are two supermodes, each living partly in both. Coupled-mode theory[14] describes this with two amplitudes: A(z) for the field in the silicon mode and B(z) for the field in the glass mode, both slowly varying along z, with the glass mode's phase factored out so that only the difference between the guides remains:

dA/dz = −i · [ k0δ(z) · A + κ · B ] dB/dz = −i · κ · A
(3)
δ(z) = neff,Si(w(z)) − neff,glass is the detuning, in index units: how far apart the two isolated guides are at the width the taper has reached at z. It is negative on the narrow side of the crossing and positive on the wide side. κ is the coupling coefficient in radians per micrometre, set by how much the two mode fields overlap across the adhesive. Thicker adhesive, smaller κ. i is the imaginary unit; the minus-i form means each amplitude advances in phase as it propagates.

With δ held fixed, equation (3) has two solutions that propagate unchanged, the supermodes, and their effective indices are

n± = neff,glass + δ/2 ± ½ √( δ² + G² ), G = 2κ/k0
(4)
G is the gap: the smallest separation the two supermodes ever reach, in index units, attained exactly at the crossing where δ = 0. Away from the crossing the curves approach the isolated ones, n+ tending to whichever isolated index is larger. The two-guide solve at the crossing gives G = 6.0 × 10−3 for this geometry, so κ = 1.22 × 10−2 rad/µm, and that single number, together with the isolated silicon curve, is all the two-mode model needs.

How much of the upper supermode lives in the silicon follows from the same algebra:

fSi = ½ · [ 1 + δ / √( δ² + G² ) ]
(5)
fSi is the fraction of the upper supermode's power carried in the silicon. Far on the narrow side (δ << −G) it is 0: the mode is the glass mode with a whisper of silicon. At the crossing it is exactly ½. Far on the wide side it is 1. At the 180 nm tip the detuning is 3.7 gaps below the crossing and fSi is 1.7%: launching the pure glass mode there mismatches the supermode by that much, and that is the floor under everything that follows.

That is the whole mechanism in one sentence: if the taper changes slowly enough, the light stays in the upper supermode, and the upper supermode turns from glass into silicon by itself. Nothing is coupled "across" in the ordinary sense. The mode the light is in simply changes its shape as the structure changes underneath it. Everything that follows is about the words "slowly enough".

The beat length

Launch light into the glass mode alone at some fixed width. The glass mode is not a supermode; it is an equal mixture of the two, and the two travel at different speeds. As they slip in phase the power sloshes from one guide to the other and back. One full slosh takes

Lb = λ / ( n+ − n− ) = λ / √( δ² + G² )
(6)
Lb is the beat length, the distance over which the two supermodes accumulate one full wave of phase difference. At the crossing it is longest, λ/G = 1.55 µm / 0.0060 = 258 µm. Full transfer at fixed geometry takes half of that, 129 µm. Away from the crossing Lb shortens but the slosh is incomplete: at 218 nm it is 74 µm long and only a small fraction of the power ever visits the other guide.

A number to hold on to: a quarter of a millimetre, at a place where the guides are a micrometre apart. The two lengths are not in competition. The adhesive thickness sets κ, κ sets G, and G sets Lb. A thicker bondline makes the slosh slower, and it is the slowness that the taper must respect.

Offered against needed

The taper on channels 13 and 14 of the die is linear: 180 nm at the tip, 500 nm at 2 mm, so it widens at 0.16 nm per micrometre. The hand-off happens where the supermode changes character, within about 20 nm either side of the crossing, and the linear taper crosses that 40 nm of width in 250 µm. Along the same stretch, the beat length from equation (6) rises from 77 µm at 183 nm through 258 µm at the crossing and falls back to 74 µm at 218 nm. The taper offers roughly one slosh where the exchange is slowest.

Adiabaticity, made a number

"Slowly enough" can be quantified. Write the upper supermode's composition as a mixing angle θ, with fSi = sin²θ, so that θ runs from 0 on the glass side to π/2 on the silicon side. The light stays in the supermode if the angle turns slowly compared with the rate at which the two supermodes drift apart in phase. The ratio is the adiabaticity parameter:[16], [17]

ε(z) = | dθ/dz | / ( k0 √( δ² + G² ) ) = G · | dδ/dz | / ( 2 k0 ( δ² + G² )3/2 )
(7)
The denominator is the local beat rate in radians per micrometre, the phase the supermodes gain on each other per unit length. The numerator is how fast the mode's composition is being asked to change. ε << 1 means the structure changes over many beat lengths and the transfer is adiabatic; ε near 1 means the structure changes within one beat and the light cannot follow. The second form uses tan 2θ = G/δ. The (δ² + G²)3/2 in the denominator is the point: at the crossing δ = 0 and ε is largest, so it is there that the taper must be slowest. And G² in that denominator is why a larger gap makes the whole problem easier: doubling G quarters ε for the same taper.

For the linear taper, dδ/dz is the slope of the silicon curve at the crossing, about 1.0 × 10−3 per nanometre of width, times the taper's 0.16 nm per micrometre, giving 1.6 × 10−4 per micrometre. Put into equation (7) at δ = 0, ε comes out at 0.53. The linear taper is on the edge: not adiabatic by the usual rule of thumb, which asks for 0.1, but not violent either.

The repair follows from asking the opposite question: what profile holds ε constant? Set ε to a chosen value in equation (7) and integrate:[17]

z(δ) = δ / ( 2 ε k0 G √( δ² + G² ) ) + constant
(8)
This gives position as a function of detuning, and through the silicon curve, as a function of width. The profile crawls through the crossing, where the bracket is smallest, and races through the wide part, where nothing is happening. Choosing ε so that the whole profile is 2 mm long gives ε = 0.020, and the taper spends 1.97 of its 2 mm between 183 and 223 nm. That is more caution than the rule of thumb asks for: the same profile held at ε = 0.1 is 404 µm long.

There is a classical result for what happens when a linear sweep passes a crossing too fast. Landau and Zener solved exactly this two-level problem in 1932, for an atom whose energy levels are swept through a crossing in time;[15] replace time with z and energy with propagation constant and the mathematics is identical. The probability that the light does not follow the supermode, and stays in the glass, is

Pstay = exp( −2π κ² / | d(k0δ)/dz | )
(9)
κ = k0G/2 is the coupling coefficient from equation (4), and the denominator is the rate at which the detuning is swept, in radians per micrometre squared. For the linear taper the exponent is 1.5, so Pstay = 0.22: the Landau-Zener estimate says 22% of the light is left behind in the glass and 78% reaches the silicon. The formula assumes the sweep runs from far below the crossing to far above it, which a taper starting 3.7 gaps below does nearly well enough.

Rung 1: integrating the two-mode equations

Equation (3) is two coupled ordinary differential equations with a z-dependent coefficient, and a numerical integrator solves them in a fraction of a second for any profile. All the light starts in the glass, B(0) = 1, A(0) = 0, and the quantity reported is |A(L)|², the power in the silicon at the end.

Three readings. The linear taper and Landau-Zener agree to within a percent, which is what the estimate should do when the sweep really does start well below the crossing. The shaped taper reaches about 98% at every length from 150 µm upward, and the bottom panel shows why it goes no higher: the 1.7% mismatch at the tip launches that much into the lower supermode, which then beats against the upper one, and the final fraction wobbles by a percent or two with length around a level the start has set. A 170 nm tip would halve the floor. Third, the shaped profile at 2 mm is far slower than it needs to be; a 400 µm shaped taper does the same job, and length on a die is not free.

What the two-mode model cannot see: radiation into the adhesive and the glass substrate, the other polarization, the glass guide's own higher modes, reflection, and the fact that κ itself changes along the taper as the silicon mode swells. It is a model of one mechanism, and it says the mechanism is marginal for the linear taper and comfortable for the shaped one. Whether the other mechanisms take a share is what rung 2 is for. Rung 2 has already earned its keep once on this page, as the next section tells.

What rung 2 caught

Stage 3 was first designed on the dashed branch of the branches figure: a two-dimensional finite-element sweep of "the fundamental silicon mode" against width,[10] which put the crossing at 156 nm with a gap of 2.8 × 10−3. On that branch the two-mode model gave 62% for a linear taper from a 130 nm tip and 94% for a constant-ε profile that spent 1.78 of its 2 mm between 140 and 162 nm. That profile was built as a three-dimensional eigenmode expansion, priced at 1.33 credits, and run on 2026-09-03.

It transferred 2.2%. The scattering matrix said where the rest went: 71.5% of the launched glass mode arrived at the far end still in the glass mode, 1.1% was reflected, and the power in the silicon grew only slowly with taper length, from 1.5% at 300 µm to 4.8% at 5 mm. A disagreement of that size is not a solver difference; it is one input being wrong, and a free local solve of the same cross-section with the same mode solver found which one. Below 220 nm the strip is taller than it is wide, the sweep had followed the vertical mode down through the swap without noticing, and the horizontal mode the glass light couples to does not cross the glass line until 203 nm. The shaped taper crawled through 140 to 162 nm, where nothing happens for that polarization, and then raced through the real crossing at 1.4 nm per micrometre, seven times faster than a linear taper. Two percent is about what a sudden crossing should give.

The same run showed a second, purely numerical, problem. The columns of a lossless scattering matrix should sum to one. For the launched mode they summed to 0.75, and for the vertically polarized glass mode to 0.28. Nothing in the model absorbs light, and a glass mode passing 120 cell boundaries untouched should lose nothing, so the missing quarter is the eigenmode basis being too small: eight modes per cell could not represent the field at each boundary, a fraction of a percent leaked into modes outside the basis at every interface, and 120 interfaces added up. This is the classic failure of the method, and it is why an eigenmode-expansion result is always run at two mode counts before it is believed. The rebuilt jobs use sixteen.

Rung 2, rebuilt: eigenmode expansion in three dimensions

Eigenmode expansion (EME) is the method built for structures like this one: long along the light, slowly varying, with nothing happening in the transverse plane that a mode solver cannot capture.[18] The taper is cut into cells along z. In each cell the cross-section is frozen and its modes are solved, all of them, glass-like, silicon-like, both polarizations, the leaky ones, up to a chosen count. Within a cell every field is a sum of those modes, each propagating with its own β:

E(x, y, z) = Σm [ am+ e−iβmz + am− e+iβmz ] · Em(x, y)
(10)
Em(x, y) is the field pattern of mode m in that cell and βm its propagation constant. am+ and am− are the amplitudes travelling forward and backward. At every boundary between cells the fields on the two sides are matched by overlap integrals, which gives a scattering matrix for that interface; the matrices of all cells and interfaces are multiplied together into one scattering matrix for the whole taper. Its element S21 between the glass mode at port 1 and the silicon mode at port 2 is the answer, and |S21|² is the transferred power. Because each cell's modes are solved once, changing the length of every cell by the same factor costs nothing new: a sweep over taper lengths comes free with a single run. And the column sums of the matrix are the built-in check on whether the basis was big enough.

The model as rebuilt: a 16 × 12 µm box in the cross-section, the glass and its guide, the adhesive, the silicon strip as a polygon whose width follows the profile, oxide above; a mesh of 20 nm on the silicon and 120 nm on the glass guide, 238 × 162 cells across; 120 slices along the 2.02 mm; sixteen modes per slice; absorbing layers on the box walls so that radiated light leaves rather than reflects; one wavelength, 1550 nm.[19] Two jobs: the shaped 180 to 500 nm taper with a sweep over seven lengths from 0.3 to 5 mm, and the linear 180 to 500 nm taper of die channels 13 and 14 with a field monitor down the middle for the picture of the light crossing the bondline.

Result: pending. The two rebuilt jobs exist as simulation files and have not yet been run; their cost estimate comes first. When they have run, this box becomes a table of transferred power against taper length for the shaped profile beside the rung-1 column above, the column sums that say whether sixteen modes were enough, the linear taper's number against its 77%, and the field picture. If the three-dimensional numbers land near the two-mode ones, the two-mode picture was adequate on this branch. If they land well below, the mechanisms it cannot see are taking their share, and that is a finding too.

What stage 3 establishes

The stage in eleven statements. If any of them is not obvious, that is the section to reread.

  1. The hand-off is not a joint. The guides run in parallel across a 1 µm bondline and the light moves through the overlap of their evanescent tails.
  2. A silicon strip narrower than it is tall has two fundamental modes, one for each polarization, and the glass light couples only to the one polarized like itself, the horizontal one.
  3. Power moves freely only where the two isolated guides are phase matched, here at a silicon width of 203 nm on the horizontal branch, and that width is set by the glass, not by the silicon.
  4. Below about 199 nm the isolated strip carries no horizontal mode at all, which is exactly what a tip should be.
  5. Solved together, the pair has two supermodes that avoid each other by a gap G at the crossing, 6.0 × 10−3 here. The light rides the upper one from glass into silicon.
  6. The beat length λ/√(δ²+G²) is the distance of one full exchange. It is longest at the crossing, 258 µm here, and it is set by the bondline, not compared with it.
  7. Whether the light stays in the supermode is decided by the adiabaticity parameter ε, the rate of change of the mode's composition over the local beat rate. A larger gap quarters it.
  8. The linear 180 nm taper of the die reaches ε = 0.53 at the crossing: marginal, and the two-mode model gives it 77%.
  9. Holding ε constant gives a profile in closed form. At 2 mm it holds 0.020 and transfers 98%; at 400 µm and ε = 0.1 it does the same.
  10. The first design followed the wrong polarization branch, and the three-dimensional solve found it: 2.2% transferred, the light still in the glass. A model that tracks one mechanism can only say that mechanism is fine.
  11. An eigenmode-expansion result is believed only when its column sums are one and it survives a second mode count.

Check yourself

Why is butt-coupling the glass guide to the silicon wire not an option?

Because the modes are 6.6 µm and 0.5 µm across, and coupling is an overlap of fields. The size mismatch alone costs 5.55 dB, before any misalignment. The evanescent hand-off avoids ever putting the two modes face to face.

A 220 nm tall strip is narrowed from 500 to 150 nm. What happens to "the fundamental mode"?

At 220 nm the strip is square and the horizontally and vertically polarized modes are degenerate. Below it the vertical one becomes the fundamental and the horizontal one falls away faster, reaching cutoff against the adhesive near 199 nm. A sweep that simply follows the lowest mode changes polarization on the way down without any warning in the numbers.

The beat length at the crossing is 258 µm. Does the adhesive have to be thinner than that?

No. The two lengths lie along different directions and are not in competition. The adhesive thickness sets the coupling κ, which sets the gap G, which sets the beat length. A thicker bondline lengthens the beat and makes the taper's job slower; it does not compete with the beat length.

Why must the taper be slowest exactly at the crossing?

Because the adiabaticity parameter carries (δ²+G²)3/2 in its denominator. At the crossing δ = 0, the supermodes are closest, the beat is slowest, and any change in the structure is a larger fraction of a beat. The same width change that is harmless at 300 nm is what matters at 203 nm.

A three-dimensional run transfers 2% where a two-mode model promised 94%. What do you check first?

Where the rest of the power went. The scattering matrix says whether it stayed in the launched mode, went to another mode, was reflected, or vanished. Here 71.5% stayed in the glass mode, which says the light never met a crossing, and 25% vanished, which says the mode basis was too small. Each of those points at a different mistake, and both were present.

Why does a length sweep in eigenmode expansion cost almost nothing extra?

Because the expensive step is solving the modes of each cell's cross-section, and scaling every cell's length by the same factor changes only the phase each mode accumulates, not the modes themselves. The cell scattering matrices are rebuilt from the same modes and multiplied again.

The shaped taper transfers 98% at 400 µm and 98% at 2 mm. Which would you put on the die?

The short one, other things being equal. Die area is not free, a longer taper has more sidewall to scatter from and more length over which the bondline must stay uniform, and the extra caution buys nothing the two-mode model can see. The 2 mm version exists because the study started from a 2 mm taper; the number to design to is ε, not the length.
