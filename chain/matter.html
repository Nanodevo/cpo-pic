Foundations: light in matter

Why a refractive index exists at all. One electron in a well, driven by the light's own field, explains the index, dispersion, absorption and the origin of nonlinearity. Every later page rests on this one.

1. The scene

Every atom is a positive nucleus with a negative electron cloud around it. With no light present the cloud is centred on the nucleus, and the atom has no dipole. Light is an oscillating electric field. As it passes, that field pushes the electron cloud one way and the nucleus the other, by an amount so small that in a fiber carrying a milliwatt of laser light it is around 10−16 m, a millionth of the width of the atom. The atom is now a tiny dipole p.

Add up all the little dipoles in a cubic metre and the total is the polarization P. It is a property of the material: how far the charges inside it have been pulled apart. And E is the field of the light itself. Nothing in the scene is charged or applying a field from outside.

2. What χ is, and what kind of statement it is

For a weak field the response is proportional to the push:

P = ε₀ · χ · E
(1)
Equation (1) is not a law of nature. It is a constitutive relation: an assumption about a particular material in a particular regime, and the only place in the whole framework where the material enters. The definitions around it, such as D ≡ ε₀E + P, cannot be wrong. Equation (1) can, and when a model disagrees with a measurement, this is the line to suspect.

Combining (1) with the definition of D gives D = ε₀(1 + χ)E, and the wave equation then propagates at speed v = c/√(1 + χ). Since the index is defined as n = c/v:

n² = εr = 1 + χ
(2)

The 1 is the vacuum, present even with no material. The χ is what the material adds. For ordinary transparent matter χ > 0, which is why the index is always greater than one.

Both sides of (2) must be evaluated at the same frequency. Water has εr ≈ 80 at DC and n² = 1.77 at optical frequencies, because whole molecules can rotate to follow a slow field and cannot follow one at 1014 Hz. The frequency dependence of χ is dispersion.

3. Why the light appears to slow down

The speed of light in vacuum, c, is constant and is never violated. A light pulse nevertheless takes measurably longer to cross a block of glass. Both are true, and two tempting pictures of why are wrong: photons do not bounce between atoms, or glass would look like milk, and atoms do not absorb and re-emit with a delay, or the beam would lose direction and phase.

What happens is interference. Between the atoms the field always travels at c. But the oscillating electrons radiate their own wave at the same frequency, and that re-radiated wave arrives a quarter cycle behind the incident one. The sum, which is what any detector sees, has almost the original shape but its peaks arrive later. Stack layer upon layer and the retardation accumulates in proportion to distance, which is indistinguishable from a wave travelling more slowly.

The light is not slowed. It is continuously rebuilt out of interference, and the rebuilt wave peaks late. The refractive index is a count of how much phase the material steals per unit distance.

Relativity constrains what can carry energy or information. A crest in an established wave is a label on a pattern, like the lighthouse spot, and carries neither. To send information something must change, a change is a new front, and fronts travel at exactly c. That is why vp = c/n is free to be less than c, or in some materials more, without troubling anyone.

4. The driven electron

Near the bottom of its binding well the electron feels a restoring force proportional to displacement, plus damping, plus the push from the light:

m·x″ + m·Γ·x′ + m·ω₀²·x = −e·E(t)
(3)

A mass on a spring being shaken. Writing E = E₀cos(ωt) and solving for the steady state with complex exponentials gives an amplitude and a lag:

x₀ = (e·E₀/m) / √[(ω₀² − ω²)² + Γ²ω²]      tan φ = Γω / (ω₀² − ω²)
(4)
  1. ω << ω₀: in phase. The electron simply follows the field. This is glass at telecom: silica's resonance is in the deep ultraviolet near 120 nm, and 1550 nm light drives it at about 8% of that frequency. Far out on the flat side of the curve, where absorption is negligible, which is why a fiber loses 0.2 dB per kilometre.
  2. ω = ω₀: a quarter cycle behind. Power delivered is force times velocity, and velocity peaks a quarter cycle after displacement, so pushing a quarter cycle ahead of the position is the condition for maximum energy transfer. That is resonance, and it is absorption.
  3. ω >> ω₀: half a cycle behind. Inertia wins; the electron moves against the field. Here χ goes negative and n dips below 1, which is why the X-ray refractive index of matter is slightly less than unity.
Where the quarter-cycle-late re-radiation of Figure 2 comes from. Radiation follows acceleration, which is half a cycle from displacement, and summing the wavelets from a whole plane of dipoles contributes a further quarter cycle from the geometry of the sum. Net: a quarter cycle behind, which is a pure phase delay with no energy exchange. At resonance the electron's own lag adds another quarter cycle and the re-radiated field is in antiphase with the incident one: cancellation, which is absorption. Same oscillator, two faces. This is the physical content of the Kramers-Kronig relations: a material cannot have dispersion without absorption somewhere in its spectrum.

And why prisms work: in the transparent window n rises with frequency because rising frequency means climbing toward the resonance. Blue is nearer the ultraviolet than red, sees a larger index, and bends more.

6. Four ways equation (1) can fail

There is one χ. Equation (1) quietly assumes that P is parallel to E, instantaneous, in phase with E, and proportional to E. Each assumption can be dropped on its own.

Assumption droppedχ becomesPhenomenon
P parallel to Ea tensor χijbirefringence, polarization dependent loss
instantaneousa function χ(ω)dispersion
in phase with Ecomplex, χ′ + iχ″absorption: n″ and α = 2k₀n″
proportional to Ea series, χ(1)E + χ(2)E² + χ(3)E³all of nonlinear optics

The superscripts in the last row are orders in a Taylor expansion, not alternatives. χ(1) is the ordinary χ; the higher terms are always present and merely too small to notice until the field is large. Their units follow from the equation: χ(2) in m/V, χ(3) in m²/V².

If the well is symmetric, V(−x) = V(x), its expansion has only even powers, the restoring force has only odd powers, and the second-order response vanishes identically: centrosymmetric materials have χ(2) = 0 exactly. Silicon, silica and glass are centrosymmetric. That single symmetry is why silicon modulators must use the plasma dispersion effect rather than the Pockels effect, and why lithium niobate exists as a rival platform.

Check yourself

P is the polarization of what, and where does E come from?

P is the polarization of the material: the dipole moment per unit volume from electron clouds displaced relative to their nuclei. E is the electric field of the light itself. Nothing else in the scene is charged.

If c is constant, in what sense does light travel slower in glass?

The phase velocity is c/n. Between atoms the field always moves at c; the re-radiated waves from the electrons add to it with a lag, and the sum peaks later at each layer. The front of a switched-on wave still travels at exactly c.

Why is the lag exactly a quarter cycle at resonance?

Power is force times velocity. Velocity peaks a quarter cycle after displacement, so the force must lead the displacement by a quarter cycle to transfer energy fastest. Resonance is that condition.

Why does silicon have no second-order nonlinearity?

Its binding well is symmetric, so the expansion of the restoring force contains no even term, and χ(2) vanishes by parity. The same argument applies to the pendulum.
