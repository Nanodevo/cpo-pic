Stage 2: waveguides in the glass

A waveguide made by changing glass that was already there, not by depositing anything. Why its index contrast has to be small, how diffusion decides its shape, and what the joint with the fiber actually costs.

The process, and what is missing from it

Look at what is not in that picture: no deposition, no etching, no second material. An alkali ion sitting in the glass network, usually sodium, is swapped for a larger one from a molten salt bath, and the region where that happened has a slightly higher refractive index. That is the entire process.

The index rises for two reasons: the larger ion is more polarizable, and forcing it into a site sized for a smaller one puts the network under stress. A potassium-for-sodium exchange gives a Δn of roughly 0.01. Silver for sodium gives ten times more, at the cost of optical loss and silver chemistry that has to be controlled.

The small Δn is the entire point

index contrastmode size
SMF-28 fiberΔn ≈ 0.006710.4 µm at 1550 nm
IOX glass waveguideΔn ≈ 0.005 to 0.016 to 10 µm
silicon waveguideΔn ≈ 20.5 µm
A twenty-fold jump in mode size sits between the fiber and the chip, and it cannot be crossed in one step without enormous coupling loss. Glass is the intermediate rung. The bridge is a mode-size transformer, and the reason it can be one is that ion exchange produces exactly the weak guiding a fiber-sized mode needs.

It is a diffusion process

The concentration of the incoming ion obeys Fick's second law, with the added complication that the diffusivity depends on local concentration, since the two species move at different rates and drag one another. The scaling that matters is the familiar one:

depth ∝ √(D · t)    D = D₀ · exp(−Ea / kT)

Depth is set by time and temperature, and quadrupling the time only doubles the depth. Typical exchanges run for hours at a few hundred degrees. Same arithmetic as a dopant drive-in, and the same Arrhenius sensitivity: a few degrees of furnace error moves the waveguide.

The waveguide is wider than the mask opening

Diffusion has no sense of direction. Ions entering through an opening of width W spread sideways under the mask at very nearly the rate they travel downward, so by the time the front reaches depth d it has also crept about 0.8d under each mask edge:

final width ≈ W + 1.6 · d

This is the same lateral diffusion that makes a doped region in silicon wider than the window opened for it. Three consequences follow.

  1. The mask opening is much narrower than the waveguide. For a guide 8 µm across diffused 5 µm deep, the opening is a couple of micrometres, not eight.
  2. Depth and width are not independent. One exchange has one knob, the product of time and temperature, and it moves both at once. The aspect ratio is set by the physics; only the overall scale is yours.
  3. Which is why burial is a separate step. Field-assisted burial is not a nicety, it is the second degree of freedom. It moves the guide down and reshapes it toward circular, giving back some of the control that isotropic diffusion took away.
A one-step surface exchange leaves a half-disc profile with its peak at the surface. Half a mode, cut off flat, couples badly to a round fiber mode, and it sits where surface scattering can reach it.

Three knobs, nearly orthogonal

KnobSets
melt compositionΔn
time × temperaturedepth and width
burial field × timehow deep, and how round

Temperature controls how far the ions get, not how many of them swap. Surface concentration, and therefore Δn, is fixed by the melt composition however hot the furnace is. To halve Δn you do not change the furnace program, you dilute the bath with sodium nitrate so the two species compete and fewer sites end up exchanged. The partition between melt and glass follows an equilibrium with its own selectivity coefficient rather than a straight line, so the exact ratio comes from calibration rather than from a formula.

Temperature does move Δn, second order, through the stress component: annealing near the strain point relaxes some of the stress-induced index rise away. That is a source of drift, not a control knob.

This near-independence is the whole reason glass ion exchange is used for fiber-matched waveguides. The index contrast can be dialled to fit a fiber mode without giving up control of the geometry. In silicon, Δn is whatever silicon and oxide happen to be.

The joint: only the mode matters

Coupling is an overlap of fields. The integral has no term for core radius. Two guides with completely different cores couple perfectly if their modes match, and two with identical cores couple badly if their modes differ.

η = 4·w₁²·w₂² / (w₁² + w₂²)²    IL = −10·log₁₀(η)

Worked at 1550 nm for a guide made with a straight potassium exchange at Δn = 0.01, taken to the largest size that is still single mode:

NA = √(2·n₁·Δn) = 0.173
a_max = 2.405·λ / (2π·NA) = 3.43 µm
w = 3.77 µm, so MFD = 7.54 µm
against the fiber's 10.4 µm: IL = 0.44 dB, with zero misalignment

Against a 0.5 dB budget for the entire connection, that design is dead before a single micrometre of tolerance has been spent. And the obvious fix does not work: forcing the core to match the fiber's 4.1 µm at Δn = 0.01 gives V = 2.88, so the guide is no longer single mode.

a and w are not independent

Once V is fixed, w/a is fixed, because that is what Marcuse's formula says. Substituting the cutoff condition into it eliminates the core radius entirely:

w_max = 1.099 × 2.405 × λ / (2π·NA) = 0.652 / NA  (µm, at λ = 1.55 µm)
The largest single-mode spot you can make depends only on NA. Widen the guide and you must weaken it by the same factor or it stops being single mode. So the knob is not size, it is Δn, and it goes down.

Match w and match V, and a matches automatically. The real design freedom is which V to operate at. Not the cutoff, where a few degrees of furnace drift tips the bridge into carrying a second mode, and not far below it, where the guide becomes too weakly confining to route. Around V = 2.0, which is where SMF-28 itself sits.

The design loop

1. the fiber gives you w from λ and the fiber spec 2. you choose V for process margin, 2.0 to 2.3 3. w and V give you a and NA because V fixes w/a 4. NA gives you Δn which is melt composition 5. a and depth give the mask and the time-temperature program 6. check the bend radius Δn allows 7. if the routing will not fit, go back to 2

Step 7 is what makes this engineering rather than arithmetic. Coupling pushes Δn down, routing pushes it up, and the design sits where they meet.

What Corning actually reports

Their published figures, which are worth holding against the derivation above. The ion-exchanged waveguides were "designed to match the mode field of a Corning SMF-28 Ultra Optical Fiber at 1310 nm", with propagation loss below 0.1 dB/cm. Fiber-to-waveguide coupling loss is quoted at 0.3 dB with air above the guide and 0.1 dB with an index-matched adhesive top cladding at 1310 nm, rising to 0.6 and 0.2 dB at 1550 nm.

Three times the loss, from nothing but what sits above the waveguide. Air makes the mode asymmetric and pulls it out of shape; matching the index restores something the fiber recognises. The adhesive in the drawing is optics, not glue.
Their work is at 1310 nm; the numbers derived above are at 1550 nm. The method is identical but the values are not: SMF-28 has MFD 9.2 µm at 1310 rather than 10.4, and V near 2.3 rather than 2.0. Do not quote the 1550 nm figures as theirs. Datacenter optics live in the O band because standard fiber has near-zero chromatic dispersion at 1310 nm, and over short reaches dispersion at high baud rate constrains the link more than loss does.

And 0.1 dB is not zero

With Δn chosen perfectly there is still residual loss from:

  1. ellipticity, since diffusion makes the guide wider than deep while the fiber mode is round
  2. profile shape, a graded index meeting a step index, so matched mode sizes still leave slightly different mode shapes
  3. Fresnel reflection at the two interfaces, which is what the index-matching adhesive suppresses
  4. alignment, which is a tolerancing problem rather than a waveguide one

Bending: why the board is a board

A bend of radius R is mathematically equivalent to a straight waveguide whose refractive index is tilted. This is a conformal transformation, not an approximation of convenience: all the curvature disappears into a ramp.

n_eq(x) = n(x) · (1 + x/R)

with x measured outward from the centre of the bend. Everything then follows by inspection. The mode has one effective index. Out on the tilted cladding the index climbs, and where it climbs to meet n_eff the light is no longer travelling through a medium slower than itself, so it stops being bound:

x_c = R · (n_eff − n_clad) / n_clad

The full derivation from Maxwell's equations, in thirteen steps with what each one means, is on its own page: From Maxwell to bend loss.

Bend loss is tunnelling

Between the edge of the core and x_c the field is evanescent; beyond it the field propagates freely. That is a potential barrier with a continuum on the far side, and the mode leaks through it exactly as an electron leaks out of a quantum well. Substituting the tilted index into the scalar wave equation gives a linear potential, whose solutions are Airy functions:

d²E/dx² = [ γ² − (2k₀²n₂²/R)·x ] · E

where γ² = β² − k₀²n₂² is the ordinary cladding decay constant of the straight guide. The bracket vanishes at x_t = γ²R/(2k₀²n₂²), which reduces to the same x_c as the geometric argument gives. The wave equation and the picture agree, which is the first thing to check.

WKB then gives the transmission through the barrier as exp(−2∫κdx), and since tunnelling is exponential in barrier width while barrier width is proportional to R:

Bend loss is exponential in bend radius. In the limit a << x_t the exponent becomes (2/3)·γ³R/β², which is Marcuse's 1976 curvature-loss result, derived here from the conformal picture alone.

Exponential means a cliff rather than a slope. Increase R slightly and the loss falls by orders of magnitude; decrease it slightly and the guide hemorrhages. That is why every platform is specified with a minimum bend radius rather than a loss-versus-radius curve: the curve exists but only one point on it is useful.

How it scales with Δn

Since γ ∝ √Δn and x_c ∝ R·Δn, the exponent goes as R·Δn^1.5. Holding the loss fixed:

R ∝ Δn^(−3/2)
Halving Δn multiplies the required bend radius by 2^1.5, about 2.8×. The mode-matching fix in the previous section, from Δn = 0.01 down to 0.005, nearly tripled the radius the bridge needs.
PlatformΔnPractical minimum bend radius
silicon≈ 2~5 µm
silicon nitride≈ 0.55~50 to 100 µm

standard SMF patch cord≈ 0.0067~30 mm

The last row is checkable against everyday objects: the minimum bend radius printed on any fiber patch cord is this physics and nothing else. And bend-insensitive fiber works by placing a low-index trench in the cladding just outside the core, which pushes the cladding index down exactly where the tilt is trying to bring it up, moving x_c outward and widening the barrier.

What that looks like at true scale

The light never encounters a bend. Locally it sees a straight waveguide whose cladding index rises very slightly outward, and that tilt is the entire physical content of the curvature. Bend loss is a slow bleed: a 90° turn at 15 mm is 23.6 mm of guide, roughly 290 of these windows in a row, each leaking a little through a barrier four core radii wide.

The consequence for the package

Sixteen channels leaving an MPO connector and fanning out to a die footprint need S-bends, and each costs a centimetre or two of substrate. The coupling requirement set Δn, Δn set the bend radius, and the bend radius set the physical size of the assembly. The glass part of a co-packaged optics package is a board rather than a chip because of one number at the fiber joint.

What limits propagation loss

Corning report below 0.1 dB/cm at 1310 nm. Worth placing that between the two things you already know: an etched silicon waveguide runs a few dB/cm, and optical fiber runs 0.2 dB per kilometre. So an ion-exchanged glass guide is roughly twenty times better than silicon and four orders of magnitude worse than fiber.

The reason it beats silicon is the same fact that opened this stage: there are no etched sidewalls. In an etched waveguide the dominant loss is scattering from sidewall roughness, and it rises steeply with index contrast because more field sits at the wall. An exchanged guide has no wall. Its boundary is a diffusion profile, which is smooth by construction. No deposition and no etching turns out to be a loss argument as much as a cost argument.

What is left, in rough order:

  1. Scattering from index inhomogeneity in the exchanged region: concentration fluctuations and residual stress.
  2. Surface scattering, if the guide sits at the polished surface. Burial moves the mode away from it, which is a second reason for the field-assisted step beyond mode symmetry.
  3. Absorption in the base glass: hydroxyl content and transition-metal impurities such as iron and copper. This is why the glass maker matters and why a co-packaged optics glass substrate is not window glass.
  4. Ion-specific absorption. Silver exchange gives a much larger Δn but silver can be reduced to metallic colloids in the glass, which absorb and scatter. Potassium is optically cleaner and is why it is used where loss matters.
  5. Bend loss on the routing.

Stress birefringence

Forcing a large ion into a site sized for a small one leaves the exchanged region in compression and its surroundings in tension. Through the photoelastic effect that stress makes the guide birefringent: TE and TM see different effective indices.

The magnitude is typically 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻₃, which against a Δn of 0.005 is between two and twenty percent of the guiding contrast itself. Not a rounding error.

Consequences, all of which are specified quantities on a real datasheet:

  1. Polarization dependent loss, because the two polarizations have different mode sizes and therefore different coupling to the fiber.
  2. Polarization dependent wavelength, if anything in the circuit is interferometric.
  3. Drifting insertion loss, since a standard fiber delivers a polarization state that wanders with temperature and handling. A polarization-dependent bridge is a bridge whose loss you cannot specify.

Three levers, each with a cost:

  1. Anneal to relax the stress, which also spreads the profile and reduces Δn, so it is not free.
  2. Bury the guide, which makes both the index profile and the stress field more symmetric.
  3. Choose the ion. Silver is much closer in size to sodium than potassium is, so it produces more index change for less stress, at the cost of the colloid problem above.

Do sixteen channels at 250 µm pitch talk to each other?

Two parallel guides exchange power through the overlap of one's evanescent tail with the other's core, and that tail decays as exp(−γx). For the glass guide solved earlier, with Δn = 0.005 at V = 2.0:

γ = k₀·√(n_eff² − n_clad²) = 0.32 µm⁻¹, so the field decay length is 1/γ = 3.1 µm.

At 250 µm separation the field is down by exp(−0.32 × 250) = exp(−80), around 10⁻³⁵, and the coupled power goes as the square of that.

So no. Not remotely. The number is so far beyond negligible that it stops being a design consideration at all. The 250 µm pitch is inherited from the fiber connector, not chosen for isolation. Guides in this platform could be packed at thirty to fifty micrometres and still be isolated over centimetre-scale parallel runs.

Which reframes what the fan-out on the glass is for. It is not there to separate channels for crosstalk reasons; it is there to convert the connector's mechanical pitch into whatever pitch the die wants, using bends whose radius is set by the physics two sections above.

One caveat: direct evanescent coupling is not the only crosstalk path. Light scattered out of a guide can travel in the glass slab itself and be recaptured elsewhere, and guides that converge at a bend approach much closer than their nominal pitch. Both are real, and neither is captured by the exponential above.

What stage 2 establishes

The whole stage in eleven statements. If any of them is not obvious, that is the section to reread.

  1. An ion-exchanged waveguide is made by changing glass that is already there, with no deposition and no etching.
  2. The index rise comes from a larger, more polarizable ion and from the stress of forcing it into a smaller site.
  3. The small Δn is the point, not a limitation: glass is the intermediate rung between a 10 µm fiber mode and a 0.5 µm chip mode.
  4. Depth follows √(Dt), and lateral diffusion makes the guide W + 1.6d wide, so depth and width are not independent.
  5. Three nearly orthogonal knobs: melt composition sets Δn, time and temperature set geometry, the burial field sets depth and symmetry.
  6. Coupling is an overlap of fields. Core radii are almost irrelevant, and matching them can even be actively wrong.
  7. Once V is fixed, w/a is fixed, so w_max = 0.652/NA and the only way to enlarge the mode is to weaken the guide.
  8. Bend loss is tunnelling through a barrier whose width grows with R, so loss is exponential in R and R ∝ Δn^(−3/2).
  9. Therefore the coupling requirement sets Δn, and Δn sets the physical size of the package.
  10. Propagation loss is low because there are no sidewalls; what remains is inhomogeneity, surface scattering, and base-glass purity.
  11. Stress makes the guide birefringent, which turns polarization into a specified quantity rather than an afterthought.

Check yourself

Why can you not simply make the glass waveguide's core the same size as the fiber's?

Because at a given Δn the core size is capped by the single-mode condition. Matching the fiber's 4.1 µm core at Δn = 0.01 gives V = 2.88, above cutoff, so the guide carries a second mode. Coupling is decided by the modes, not the cores, and the core is not free to move independently.

You need to halve Δn. Do you change the furnace or the bath?

The bath. Temperature sets how far the ions diffuse, not how many sites exchange. Dilute the melt with sodium nitrate so the two species compete.

Why is a multimode bridge worse than a lossy one?

Because the loss is unstable. Power divides between LP01 and LP11 depending on bend, temperature and how the fiber was placed, and only the LP01 share enters the chip. A link whose insertion loss wanders cannot be specified.

The mask opening is 2 µm and the exchange runs to 5 µm depth. How wide is the guide?

About 2 + 1.6×5 = 10 µm. Lateral diffusion under the mask edges dominates the final width once the depth exceeds the opening.

Why is bend loss exponential in bend radius rather than proportional to it?

Because it is a tunnelling process. The mode escapes through a barrier between the core edge and the radiation caustic, the barrier width grows in proportion to R, and tunnelling probability falls exponentially with barrier width.

An ion-exchanged guide runs at 0.1 dB/cm while an etched silicon guide runs at a few dB/cm. Why?

No etched sidewalls. Sidewall roughness scattering dominates loss in etched waveguides and rises steeply with index contrast. A diffused boundary is smooth by construction.

Would sixteen channels at 250 µm pitch interfere with each other?

No, by an enormous margin. The field decay length is about 3 µm, so at 250 µm the overlap is of order exp(−80). The pitch comes from the fiber connector, not from isolation.

You halve Δn to improve fiber coupling. What have you just done to the substrate size?

Increased the minimum bend radius by about 2.8×, since R scales as Δn^(−3/2). Every S-bend in the fan-out grows accordingly, and the board grows with them.
